## Abstract

Autoregressive graph generation typically tokenizes a graph by flattening it into a single linear walk over its nodes and edges. This works on small molecules but breaks down on large biological graphs that are simultaneously high in node count and *geometrically complex* (rich in higher-order substructure). We argue that flat walks teach the model to write *letters* when it should be writing *words*, and we study how to expose hierarchy in the token stream itself. We compare flat walks (SENT) against two ways of exposing hierarchy: *declaring* it as an explicit community catalog plus a separate bipartite wiring section (HSENT), or *traversing* it with a single depth-first walk so cross-community edges become ordinary back-edges (HDT). Across eight benchmarks the message is consistent: on small or geometrically simple graphs flat walks and hierarchy are within noise, but on geometrically complex large graphs (Cyclic-Peptides, Proteins) flat walks either lose basic validity outright (Proteins) or stay valid but deviate on distributional metrics (Cyclic-Peptides), while HDT stays on-distribution throughout. Declaring the hierarchy explicitly leads to drops in performance versus the flat baseline. Hierarchy must therefore be *traversed*, not merely *declared*. We further show that the coarse-before-fine structure of HDT enables motif-conditioned generation without any architectural change: providing the motif's community token prefix as context, HDT achieves near-perfect motif retention and full V.U.N. across all six scaffold types on COCONUT and GuacaMol, while flat walks collapse to near-zero uniqueness under complex-motif priming.
